Principal UX Designer · King (Activision Blizzard) · 2023-Present

I led the end-to-end UX strategy, research, and design for Workspace Management inside King's internal platform, helping workspace owners manage access, applications, and permissions directly without relying on central platform support.

Platform Scale

296 workspaces, 68 integrated applications, 1,489 MAU, and 26 active Workspace Owners across King's internal platform ecosystem.

Core Focus

Reframing IAM from a centralized admin workflow into a scalable self-service system for workspace owners and game teams.

Context

King's IUP portal is the internal platform powering game operations, developer tooling, and live-service management across all King titles. Candy Crush, Farm Heroes, and more than 30 adjacent tools depend on it. All integrated applications and 1,489 monthly active users rely on the platform for managing who can access what, govern what, and why.

Originally, access and identity management was handled centrally by platform admins. Every access request funneled through one team. That worked at small scale, but it did not survive growth.

To address that, we introduced the Workspace Owner role: game-team representatives who could manage access directly through a Workspace Management application. The idea was right, but the execution exposed a new class of usability, trust, and governance problems that were brought into scope.

My role: Principal UX Designer. I led UX strategy, research, and design for Workspace Management across multiple iterations, partnering closely with PMs, engineers, and the 26 active workspace owners who were our primary users.

Chapter 1

Fixing the Access Request Bottleneck

Phase Placeholder · Access Requests
Replace with consolidated request review interface, requester profile card, or permission-visibility flow.

The Problem

Despite having a self-service tool, pending access requests were piling up, especially for the largest game titles. End-users were blocked from doing their work, support tickets kept climbing, and the tool meant to empower workspace owners was frustrating both sides.

Through contextual interviews and a survey with workspace owners and end-users, I identified the root causes.

For Workspace Owners

  • No visibility into who was requesting access, forcing owners to message requesters on Slack just to understand the request.
  • Opaque groups and permission structures, with no easy way to see what each group actually granted.
  • Inconsistent naming conventions that made group selection guesswork.
  • Managing requests across multiple games amplified confusion because each title carried a different permission model.

For End-Users

  • Requests sat in limbo for days or weeks.
  • There was no feedback on whether a request was seen, pending, or stuck.
  • The fallback was asking for help in the platform channel, increasing load on the platform team.

Design Rationale: Surface context, don't add steps

I chose Option B: surface the right information in the current flow. Workspace owners were not confused about the process; they were missing the context needed to make a decision. The fix was information architecture, not workflow architecture.

What I Designed

  • Consolidated decision view: pending requests and group list shown side by side.
  • Requester profile card: name, team, and role surfaced from Slack integration.
  • Inline permission visibility: owners could inspect included apps before granting access.
  • Deliberate non-automation: no smart recommendation yet, because workspace owners needed to understand the system first.
The consolidated view with pending requests plus group list was the most appreciated change. Workspace owners specifically wanted to see requester identity before making access decisions.
Chapter 2

Scaling to Application Management

Phase Placeholder · Application Management
Replace with policy-editing screen, application entitlement overview, or role-flow concept.

The Evolution

The access-request improvements shaped and delivered measurable results. But as the platform grew from 40 to 68 integrated applications, workspace owners needed to manage not only who had access, but which applications were available and what permission those applications required.

Design Rationale: Three key decisions

1. Progressive filtering for role discovery

I designed a sequence of Scope -> Application -> Role. This reduced cognitive load from scanning hundreds of roles to evaluating only the few that were relevant.

2. Fix the save interaction

I recommended treating the explicit Add & Save version first. It gave users clearer control and reduced the risk of accidental permission changes.

3. Show application status inline

Instead of splitting enabled and disabled applications into separate tabs, I designed a status indicator directly in the application list.

Designing for Scale: Patterns Over One-offs

A recurring theme in this work was the difference between solving one problem and establishing a pattern that solves a whole category of problems. The shipped features improved the metrics, and the patterns became infrastructure the team could keep building on.

Reflection

What worked well

  • Embedding developers in research sessions accelerated alignment.
  • Leading with information, not workflow changes, proved right in Chapter 1.
  • Maintaining a continuous research loop meant every round informed the next iteration.

What I'd do differently

  • Quantify the problem earlier.
  • Test the notification model sooner.
  • Push harder on smart defaults.
